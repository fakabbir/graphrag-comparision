\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We measured three retrieval architectures over one corpus and one question set:
167{,}050 SEC filings from 10{,}511 companies, 508{,}714 embedded chunks and a
graph of 430{,}257 nodes and 561{,}144 edges, queried with 20 questions in four
shape categories, each asked three times, for 180 attempts scored
programmatically rather than by an LLM. GraphRAG solved 16 of 20 questions on all
three attempts, text-to-SQL 10 and vector RAG 3.

The aggregate is the least useful number in the paper. GraphRAG lost structured
aggregation outright and expensively, and the defensible summary is a rule about
question shape rather than a winner:

\begin{itemize}
  \item If the answer lies in one document and the entity is named, the binding
        constraint is \emph{entity filtering}, not ranking. Similarity search
        alone fails at scale --- 3 of 15 here --- and needs either a metadata
        filter on a stable identifier or a traversal to supply one.
  \item If the question is a \texttt{GROUP BY} over typed columns, use SQL. A
        traversal adds no information, adds a place to go wrong, and cost 12.2
        times the tokens to score 6 of 15 against 15 of 15.
  \item If the question requires resolving \emph{who} an entity is, or joining
        facts that separate documents assert, structural retrieval is worth its
        cost: 14 of 15 and 15 of 15 on the two such categories, against 0 of 15
        and 14 of 15 for the strongest baseline.
  \item If provenance must be auditable, the graph's advantage is categorical
        rather than marginal. It was never uncited across 60 attempts, because
        the traversal returns accession numbers before any text is read, whereas
        the two baselines were right-but-unciteable 10 and 12 times.
\end{itemize}

The safety finding is narrower but sharper than the accuracy finding, and it is
the one we did not anticipate. All six confident falsehoods were text-to-SQL
conflating distinct people who share a surname; GraphRAG produced none. The two
do not move together: describing the baseline's schema more completely is an
unambiguous capability improvement, and it converted honest refusals into
assertive wrong answers on exactly the questions where identity is ambiguous
(\Cref{sec:failures}). Resolving identity to an identifier before reading any
text prevents that class of error structurally rather than probabilistically. For an
application where a confident wrong answer costs more than a refusal, that
property may matter more than the pass rate.

We claim none of this beyond one corpus, one model and twenty questions. During
calibration a single undocumented schema convention proved worth three of the
four categories (\Cref{sec:harness}), which is the strongest caution we can offer
about how much weight any one benchmark run should carry --- including this one.

\subsection*{Future work}

Four steps would materially strengthen these results. \emph{Re-run all 180
attempts per model}, turning the cross-model probe of \Cref{tab:crossmodel} into a
measurement and separating architectural effects from model effects.
\emph{Repair the entity-resolution layer} --- \texttt{:RESOLVES\_TO} still covers
0.12\% of subsidiary nodes --- and establish how much of the T4 result depends on
it. \emph{Obtain an independent question set}, since the present one was written
by the author of the graph, which remains the study's most serious threat. And
\emph{vary the retriever}: a longer-context encoder with a metadata filter on CIK
would test directly whether the single-document collapse is a property of
embeddings or of this particular configuration --- our own diagnosis predicts the
metadata filter, not the encoder, does the work.
